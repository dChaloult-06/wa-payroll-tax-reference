Washington has no personal state income tax. Payroll obligations center on social insurance programs, workers' compensation, and — for Seattle employers — specific local payroll expense taxes. Local Taxes — Seattle & King County

Washington does not impose traditional local earned income taxes via payroll withholding. However, Seattle imposes specific employer-expense payroll taxes that apply to larger employers and high-wage employees. No employee withholding is required for these programs.

No local income tax withholding in Washington. Unlike Pennsylvania, Kentucky, and Ohio, Washington jurisdictions do not require employers to withhold local income taxes from employee wages. Seattle's programs below are employer-expense taxes, not employee withholding obligations.

What Else Activates When You Register?

Registering with ESD for SUI triggers:

  • →EAF assessment on the same $78,200 wage base (appears on same tax rate notice)
  • →PFML reporting obligations through the ESD shared system
  • →WA Cares Fund collection and remittance (same quarterly ESD report as of July 2023)

Registering with L&I for Workers' Comp triggers:

  • →Separate account from ESD — do not conflate L&I and ESD registrations
  • →Risk class assignment based on industry and job duties
  • →Hourly premium obligations beginning first day of employment
Important — Business Nexus Consideration
Registering for Washington payroll taxes when not mandatory creates a business connection (nexus) to Washington that may trigger other business tax obligations — including B&O tax through the Department of Revenue — even if the payroll registration itself is voluntary. Consult a qualified tax advisor before voluntarily registering in any new state to understand the full scope of obligations that may follow.

Reciprocity — Washington State

Washington currently has no personal income tax and therefore no formal income tax reciprocity agreements with other states. This creates specific multi-state scenarios that payroll professionals need to navigate carefully. Beginning January 1, 2028, SB 6346 establishes Washington's first individual income tax and grants DOR authority to negotiate reciprocity agreements — see the Individual Income Tax (2028) tab.

Why Washington has no reciprocity agreements currently: Reciprocity agreements allow residents of one state to be taxed only in their state of residence, not the state where they work. Because Washington currently imposes no personal income tax, there is nothing to reciprocate — WA residents working in another state owe no WA income tax regardless, and non-residents working in WA have no WA income tax liability either.
SB 6346 — New Reciprocity Authority Effective January 1, 2028: The Washington Millionaires' Tax (Chapter 238, Laws of 2026; Title 82A RCW) grants the WA Department of Revenue explicit authority to enter into reciprocity agreements with other states for personal services income. Sec. 203(2) provides that if another state exempts WA residents from income tax on personal services income earned there, DOR may enter a reciprocal agreement providing a similar exemption for that state's residents on personal services income earned in Washington. No agreements have been established as of this writing. Monitor DOR guidance for any announcements before the January 1, 2028 effective date.

PFML & SUI Multi-State Sourcing — The 4-Part Test

For PFML and SUI, Washington follows the federal four-part test to determine which state has jurisdiction. Apply each test in order — stop at the first test that produces a definitive answer.

Test (Apply in Order)RuleWA Applies If…
1. LocalizationWhere is the work primarily performed?Employee's work is localized to WA
2. Base of OperationsWhere does the employee start/end their work?Employee's base is in WA
3. Direction & ControlWhere is the employer directing work from?Direction/control emanates from WA
4. Employee ResidenceWhere does the employee live?Employee resides in WA (tiebreaker)
Practical application: For a fully remote employee hired to work from their WA home, Test 1 (localization) produces a clear answer — WA PFML and SUI apply. For a traveling employee who splits time across states, Test 2 or 3 determines jurisdiction. Only one state collects SUI for any given employee.

Glossary

Key terms used throughout this reference. Definitions reflect how terms are applied in Washington payroll tax compliance contexts.

Wage Base
The maximum amount of an employee's annual wages subject to a specific tax. Once an employee's wages reach the wage base for the year, that tax stops being collected. Washington's SUI wage base is $78,200 in 2026; Social Security's is $184,500. WA Cares has no wage base — it applies to all gross wages.
Experience Rating
A method of setting employer SUI tax rates based on the employer's own history of unemployment claims. Employers with fewer claims generally receive lower rates; those with more claims pay higher rates within the statutory range (0.27%–6.03% in WA).
Premium vs. Tax
Washington's PFML and WA Cares assessments are technically "premiums" — payments into an insurance fund — rather than taxes. This distinction affects how they are treated for federal tax purposes and is the reason behind the HB 2345 reallocation between family and medical leave shares.
Nexus
A sufficient connection between a business and a state that creates a legal obligation to register, file, and pay taxes in that state. For payroll purposes, nexus is typically established when an employer has an employee working in the state.
Reciprocity Agreement
A formal agreement between two states allowing residents of each state to pay income tax only in their state of residence, regardless of where they work. Washington has no such agreements because it has no personal income tax.
Four-Part Localization Test
The standard test used to determine which state has SUI and PFML jurisdiction for an employee working in multiple states. Applied in order: (1) localization of work, (2) base of operations, (3) direction and control, (4) employee residence.
EAF (Employment Administration Fund)
A small Washington employer assessment added to the SUI rate, currently approximately 0.03%. It appears as a separate line on the ESD annual tax rate determination notice and shares the same $78,200 wage base as SUI.
Voluntary Contribution
An optional additional SUI payment an employer can make to the state to reduce their experience-rated tax rate for the following year. Washington permits voluntary contributions and they can be a cost-effective strategy for employers near a rate tier boundary.
SOC Code
Standard Occupational Classification code — a federal system for classifying workers by job type. Washington ESD requires a valid SOC code for every employee on quarterly wage reports. Filings submitted without valid SOC codes are rejected.
Supplemental Pension Fund (SPF)
A component of Washington's workers' compensation premium structure, set at $0.1912 per hour worked in 2026, split equally between employer ($0.0956) and employee ($0.0956). Separate from the Medical Aid Fund and other L&I components.
PFML Voluntary Plan
An employer-sponsored paid leave plan approved by ESD as an alternative to the state PFML program. Must provide benefits at least as generous as the state plan. Employers with approved voluntary plans do not remit to the state ESD fund.
JumpStart Payroll Expense Tax
Seattle's employer-paid payroll tax applying to businesses with annual payrolls of $9,074,409 or more (2026), on individual employee compensation of $194,452 or more. Rates range from 0.746% to 2.557% based on a two-dimensional matrix of macro-payroll tier and compensation band. An employer expense — not withheld from employees.
WA Cares Fund
Washington's mandatory, publicly funded long-term care insurance program — the first of its kind in the United States. Funded by a 0.58% employee payroll deduction on all gross wages with no cap. Benefits of up to $36,500 (inflation-adjusted) became available statewide July 1, 2026.
B&O Tax
Washington's Business & Occupation tax — a gross receipts tax on business revenue, administered by the WA Department of Revenue. Not a payroll tax and not withheld from employee wages, but relevant when analyzing the full cost of doing business in Washington and when evaluating voluntary payroll registration (which may trigger B&O review).
Delinquent SUI Rate
A penalty tax rate assigned by ESD to employers with missing quarterly returns or unresolved tax balances. Calculated as Rate Class 40 base (5.40%) + delinquency surcharge (2.00%) + delinquent social cost cap (0.75%) = 8.15%. With EAF: 8.18% all-in. Authority: RCW 50.29.025(2)(d).
Social Housing Tax
A 5% flat Seattle municipal excise tax on individual employee compensation exceeding $1,000,000, established by Proposition 1A (voter-approved February 2025). Effective January 1, 2025. No minimum business payroll threshold — unlike JumpStart. Employer obligation only; no employee withholding. Transitions to quarterly filing beginning 2026.

Individual Income Tax — Effective January 1, 2028

Washington enacted its first individual income tax on March 30, 2026. This tab covers what payroll professionals and employers need to know about the law as enacted — its structure, employer obligations, and implementation timeline. This tax is not yet in effect.

Not Yet In Effect

Chapter 238, Laws of 2026 (ESSB 6346), signed by Governor Ferguson on March 30, 2026. Codified as Title 82A RCW. Administered by the WA Department of Revenue — not ESD or L&I. This reference reflects the law as enacted; rulemaking by DOR is ongoing. Monitor DOR guidance through 2027 for implementation details.
SB 6346 — Bill Summary Session Law (PDF) WA DOR
Anticipated Legal Challenge: SB 6346 is expected to face constitutional litigation before its January 1, 2028 effective date. The Washington State Constitution has historically been interpreted to prohibit a graduated income tax. The bill's drafters framed this tax as an "excise tax on the receipt of income" — the same approach used for the WA Capital Gains Tax (RCW 82.87), which the WA Supreme Court upheld in Quinn v. State (2023). A challenge to SB 6346 is likely to follow a similar legal path. The effective date of January 1, 2028 could be affected by injunctive relief or a court ruling. Employers and employees should monitor developments and avoid system configuration changes that are difficult to reverse until legal status is resolved.

Tax structure — not a flat tax: The 9.90% rate applies only to Washington taxable income — defined as Washington base income after the $1,000,000 standard deduction. The practical effect is a 0% rate on all income up to $1M, and 9.90% on every dollar above $1M. Statutorily, this is a single rate applied to a deduction-adjusted base; functionally it is a threshold-based tax on millionaires, not a flat tax on all income.
Marriage penalty — Sec. 314: Spouses and state registered domestic partners share a combined $1,000,000 standard deduction regardless of whether they file jointly or separately. Two single individuals each earning $700,000 owe $0 tax. If they marry, their $1,400,000 combined income receives only a $1,000,000 combined deduction — making $400,000 taxable at 9.90% = approximately $39,600. This structural feature is explicit in the statute and is material for high-earning couples in Washington.

Withholding rules are pending, not optional: Sec. 501(5) requires DOR to adopt rules for estimated tax payments on wages subject to federal income tax withholding. Once issued, these rules could create employer withholding obligations similar to federal income tax withholding. Employers with high-earning employees (>$1M annually) in Washington should track DOR rulemaking closely in 2027.
Nonresident employee note (Sec. 401(3)): Nonresident employees who perform services in Washington for five or fewer days cumulatively in a calendar year have no Washington-source income allocated under this tax. The 5-day de minimis rule does not apply to professional athletes, student athletes, or entertainers. This sourcing rule is relevant for employers managing multi-state employees with occasional WA work days.
Legal uncertainty — system implementation timing: Given the anticipated constitutional challenge (see above), employers should avoid irreversible payroll system configuration changes until the legal status of SB 6346 is resolved. The January 1, 2028 effective date could be delayed or invalidated by court action.

About This Tool

About This Tool

A practitioner-built reference for payroll professionals—source-linked, structured, and designed to translate complex statutory requirements into clear, operational guidance.

Purpose & Origin

Statutes, agency rules, and employer guidance are publicly available, but critical compliance details are often fragmented across multiple sources or embedded within costly software platforms. This tool consolidates Washington payroll tax requirements in the way practitioners evaluate them: defining employer obligations, identifying wage bases, clarifying nexus and sourcing rules, and linking directly to primary authority.

This reference was developed through detailed analysis of Seattle's payroll tax framework, including the implementation of Proposition 1A (Social Housing Tax). Mapping these requirements required extensive review of RCWs, WACs, and agency guidance. The result is a structured, repeatable reference designed to support accurate compliance and system implementation.

Each rate, wage base, and threshold links directly to its source. The intent is not to replace primary law, but to make it immediately accessible and verifiable for practitioners.
